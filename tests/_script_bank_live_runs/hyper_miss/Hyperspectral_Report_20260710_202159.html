1. Synthesized Scientific Analysis

This study probes the infrared plasmonic behavior of a self-assembled monolayer of fluorine- and tin-co-doped indium oxide (FT:IO) nanocrystals using monochromated low-loss electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) over a 120 × 100 nm field of view. The spectral window analyzed (0.2–1.13 eV) sits directly in the near-/mid-infrared, which is the characteristic region where localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPRs) appear in degenerately doped metal-oxide nanocrystals. Two complementary analysis approaches were combined: an exploratory statistical decomposition (PCA) to identify the dominant spectral variance patterns, and a physics-based per-pixel Lorentzian fit of the plasmon peak to extract quantitative, physically interpretable maps. **Exploratory decomposition (PCA).** Because PCA identifies directions of maximum variance rather than physically pure chemical phases, its components are best read as complementary 'views' of the data rather than as standalone materials or physical eigenmodes. Component 1 captures a strong, roughly symmetric peak near 0.45–0.50 eV — the dominant dipolar LSPR mode of the nanocrystals — and its spatial map highlights a localized bright cluster (likely a thicker or aggregated region) superimposed on a periodic modulation that traces the nanocrystal packing. Component 2 shows a narrower, slightly lower-energy peak whose spatial map is dominated by a sharp left-to-right boundary, indicating a systematic spatial gradient in the sample rather than a distinct phase — most plausibly a change in effective medium, film thickness, or packing density across the field of view. Component 3 is double-peaked (peaks near ~0.37 and ~0.60 eV) and localizes to the nanocrystal edges, necks, and interstitial network in a genuinely structured (non-noise) pattern; this edge/neck localization is consistent with near-field plasmon coupling between adjacent particles, but it could equally arise from a spatial mixture of the red- and blue-shifted plasmon populations already seen in the Peak_Center gradient, concentrated at edges where signal and geometry differ. Because PCA components are variance directions rather than physical eigenmodes, a two-peaked loading does not by itself demonstrate mode hybridization; a definitive assignment would require electromagnetic simulation (e.g., EELS/BEM/DDA). Component 4 is a tentative, low-confidence component: its spectrum is very weak (~0.03 max, roughly 3× weaker than Component 1) with a visibly noisy baseline below 0.5 eV, and its abundance map is largely a mottled/salt-and-pepper pattern with only weak dot-like features embedded in noise. At these energies (0.70–0.87 eV), this component may partly reflect the noisier low-signal high-energy tail and/or PCA variance-ordering artifacts, and it is not interpreted as a confident higher-order single-particle mode. The reconstruction quality was good in the well-resolved 0.4–0.9 eV window, but the low-energy tail (<0.3 eV) is noisier owing to residuals from zero-loss peak removal, so features there are treated cautiously. **Physics-based validation (per-pixel Lorentzian fits).** The custom analysis directly fits the plasmon peak in each pixel, converting the abstract PCA variance directions into quantitative, physically meaningful maps of resonance energy (Peak_Center), linewidth (FWHM), and signal strength (Peak_Amplitude). Because the raw data are of very low signal quality, pixels lacking sufficient signal were deliberately excluded (shown as blank/white) rather than fit to noise, which increases confidence in the retained values. These maps corroborate and sharpen the PCA picture. The Peak_Center map (mean ≈ 0.44 eV, range 0.40–0.47 eV) reveals a continuous spatial gradient: the resonance energy is higher (~0.46–0.47 eV) across most of the film and red-shifts distinctly toward the right/lower-right region (~0.41 eV). This gradient precisely reproduces the boundary captured by PCA Component 2, confirming that the split between Components 1 and 2 reflects a genuine, continuous shift in plasmon energy across the sample rather than an artifact. The Peak_Center histogram shows a sharp peak near 0.46 eV with a broad low-energy tail; this shape is consistent with the smooth gradient itself (a large, relatively uniform high-energy area plus the lower-energy gradient region) and should not be over-interpreted as evidence for two discrete, distinct plasmonic regimes. **Unified physical model.** Together, the datasets describe an ordered FT:IO nanocrystal monolayer whose infrared plasmonic response varies systematically with local structure and position. At the single-particle level, individual cores support the dominant dipolar LSPR (~0.44–0.47 eV). At particle contacts (edges, necks, interstitial gaps), the edge-localized, double-peaked spectral signature (Component 3) is consistent with near-field coupling producing hybridized, split-mode responses, though this mechanistic assignment requires electromagnetic simulation for confirmation and cannot be established by PCA alone. Superimposed on this local structure is a mesoscale gradient: a smooth red-shift of the resonance across the field of view, accompanied by qualitative variations in linewidth (FWHM, mean ≈ 0.15 eV). While linewidth relates to total plasmon damping, we treat the spatial FWHM variation as a qualitative observation only, because it can be substantially influenced by instrumental and geometric factors (ZLP-removal residuals, thickness/multiple-scattering broadening, and FWHM–amplitude fit degeneracy in low-SNR pixels); notably, the low-FWHM region coincides with the low Peak_Center region and the map edge, so we do not attribute it to intrinsic carrier mobility. The amplitude map (mean ≈ 83 a.u.) shows its highest values in the bright cluster that coincides with Abundance Map 1, most defensibly interpreted as a thicker/aggregated region, since in EELS low-loss amplitude scales with local thickness and collection geometry as much as with intrinsic material density. Importantly, the right/lower-right region appears self-consistently across Peak_Center (red-shift), FWHM (narrowing), and Peak_Amplitude, and this region coincides with the edge of the field of view. Because three nominally 'independent' maps all key off the same spatial boundary, this pattern may reflect a single shared systematic (edge-of-field / thickness / ZLP-residual) variable rather than three independent physical confirmations, and should be interpreted with that caveat. In short, the plasmonic energy landscape can be described by two length scales: possible interparticle near-field coupling at the nanoscale (edge-localized Component 3, pending simulation) and a smooth environmental/packing (or thickness) gradient at the mesoscale. 3. Key Scientific Claims

Claim 1: The localized surface plasmon resonance energy of a doped indium oxide nanocrystal monolayer varies as a continuous spatial gradient across the film, indicating position-dependent effective-medium or packing effects rather than discrete chemical phases.

Impact: Demonstrating a smooth, mesoscale gradient in plasmon energy would show that nanocrystal packing density and local dielectric environment can be used to tune infrared plasmonic response across a device area, which is important for designing spatially graded plasmonic and smart-window coatings.
