1. Synthesized Scientific Analysis

### Objective and Approach The goal of this study was to locate a sputtered gold (Au) layer and, if possible, quantify its thickness using spectral X-ray transmission imaging. The physical signature of interest is the Au K absorption edge at 80.8 keV, where the transmitted X-ray intensity drops sharply as gold begins to absorb strongly above that energy. Because the measurement is a transmission experiment, a companion flat-field reference (I0) was supplied so that per-pixel absorption could be computed against the incident beam profile rather than being confounded by variations in source intensity across the field of view. ### Physics-Based Mapping of the Au K-Edge Rather than rely on a purely statistical decomposition (which would be prone to entangling the sharp Au edge with the much larger broadband transmission gradient of the substrate and beam), the analysis used a custom, physics-driven method. For every pixel, the size of the absorption jump across the Au K-edge was measured directly by comparing narrow, symmetric energy windows placed just below (~80.0 keV) and just above (~81.6 keV) the edge. This isolates the sharp discontinuity that is unique to gold and rejects the slowly varying background transmission. Pixels were only accepted if they showed a genuine positive jump above the noise, met a minimum signal-quality threshold, and produced an absorption value physically consistent with a thin (≤5 µm) sputtered film. ### Localization: Where the Gold Is (Tentative) The edge-step map (Au_Edge_Step) shows accepted pixels distributed across a curved, semicircular footprint in the upper portion of the field of view. However, within that footprint the accepted pixels appear as isolated, randomly scattered points (salt-and-pepper speckle) rather than a filled, connected material region. The semicircular boundary is most plausibly the outline of the accepted-pixel region — i.e., an ROI/thresholding acceptance mask or beam/sample-mount edge — rather than a resolved gold film. Because the per-pixel edge-step values sit close to the intrinsic noise floor (see thickness section), this speckled interior is exactly the pattern that noise would produce, so the step map is suggestive at best and cannot be treated as strong standalone evidence of localization. The companion signal-quality map (Au_Edge_SNR) is the only genuinely structured image in the dataset: it shows a bright, high-value upper region and a smooth, dark semicircular lobe in the lower field. Importantly, this structure is essentially the inverse of the claimed gold footprint. Two interpretations are consistent with the data and cannot be distinguished from these maps alone: 1. **Gold in the upper region:** the upper field shows a stronger (though noisy) edge signal. 2. **Attenuation-shadow / open-beam interpretation:** the smooth dark lower lobe is the actual attenuating sample (lower transmitted flux producing the visible dark disc), while the upper region is open beam where the edge-step ratio is high but dominated by noisy statistics. The physical assignment of "gold = upper region" is therefore not established by the data and may be reversed. The physical reason is straightforward: at 80.8 keV the incident flux is low (on the order of a hundred counts per energy channel, visible in the steep high-energy fall-off of the reference spectrum), so the statistical uncertainty on any individual pixel's edge height is large. As a consequence, although the average edge step can in principle be converted into an approximate film thickness using the known Au mass-attenuation discontinuity and gold's density (19.32 g/cm³), the absolute thickness value cannot be verified and should be reported only as an order-of-magnitude estimate, if at all. The edge-step histogram is not cleanly unimodal: it shows a prominent spike in the near-zero bin (~0.00–0.005) that is separate from the broader distribution centered near ~0.025. A spike at zero is a recognized signature of noise/floor pixels rather than a physically reasonable single-population distribution.
