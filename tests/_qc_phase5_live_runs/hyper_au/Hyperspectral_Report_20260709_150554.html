1. Synthesized Scientific Analysis

### Overview and Objective The goal of this study was to detect the presence of gold (Au) within a spectral X-ray transmission dataset and to estimate its thickness from the characteristic gold K-edge, an abrupt jump in X-ray absorption occurring at approximately 80.7 keV. The dataset consists of an 80 x 80 pixel image (covering a 20 x 20 mm field of view) with a full X-ray energy spectrum recorded at each pixel. A companion 'blank' measurement acquired without the sample (the flat-field, or I0) was provided so that true absorbance could be computed at every pixel. ### Analytical Strategy Because the scientific question is quantitative and element-specific, the analysis was built directly around the physics of X-ray transmission rather than on generic statistical decomposition. For every pixel, the absorbance (optical depth) was calculated by comparing the sample measurement against the blank baseline using the standard relationship, absorbance = ln(I0/I). Low-energy X-rays below 40 keV were deliberately excluded, since at these energies the high-power bremsstrahlung source produces signal dominated by scatter and noise when passing through a high-atomic-number metal like gold — a well-established limitation in the literature. The gold-specific fingerprint, the K-edge, was then isolated by comparing the absorbance in narrow energy windows just below and just above 80.7 keV. The size of this discontinuity (the edge jump) is proportional to the amount of gold along the X-ray path, which allows it to be converted into a physical thickness using tabulated reference values for gold's attenuation and density. ### Presence of Gold Across the Field of View The edge-jump map shows a strongly positive K-edge signal across essentially every pixel, clustering around a mean value of roughly 0.48 (in optical-depth units) with a standard deviation of about 0.08 — a relative spread of ~17%. The accompanying histogram shows a single dominant population with only a negligible tail near zero. Importantly, the spatial map is dominated by random pixel-to-pixel speckle and does NOT resolve any structured spatial features: there is no visible coupon boundary, edge, or object outline. Consequently, the analysis cannot localize the gold as a distinct object; it can only assert that a positive K-edge signal is present everywhere in the imaged area. A uniform positive edge-jump across the whole field is consistent with a gold-bearing material filling the field of view, but because no object boundary is resolved, this measurement effectively yields a global average rather than a localized detection. Because no zero-gold background region is visible anywhere in the map, the data cannot be tested against a true 'no-gold' reference within this dataset. This limits the specificity of the K-edge attribution: these maps alone do not exclude the possibility that part of the signal reflects a general attenuation offset rather than exclusively the Au K-edge.
