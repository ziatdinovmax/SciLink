1. Synthesized Scientific Analysis

### Overview and Objective The goal of this study was to locate a sputtered gold (Au) layer and, if possible, quantify its thickness using spectral X-ray transmission imaging. Gold has a distinctive absorption feature—the Au K-edge at 80.8 keV—where the material abruptly becomes more absorbing to X-rays. By converting the transmitted X-ray intensity into optical depth (using the provided flat-field baseline as the incident-beam reference) and measuring the size of the absorption jump across this edge, we intended to both map where the Au is located and, in principle, estimate how thick it is. As detailed below, the analysis succeeded only in localizing a high-attenuation region; attribution of that region specifically to Au, and any thickness estimate, remain unverified. ### Spatial Localization of a High-Attenuation Region (High Confidence for the Region; Au Attribution Unverified) The physics-based edge-step map provides a clear and spatially coherent boundary. The map reveals an elevated-signal upper region and a well-defined lower boundary with a distinctive curved (dome-like) shape, and this boundary is reproduced consistently across the derived maps. We can therefore localize a high-attenuation/high-signal region with high confidence. However, the nature of this region must be stated with care. The spatial map does not show a sharp two-phase interface; instead it shows a smooth, continuous gradient from ~0 to ~2 OD across the curved boundary. The value distribution being split into a large near-zero population and a high-value population is most simply explained by geometry: the near-zero peak corresponds to the substrate/empty region, and the high-value cluster corresponds to the thick-sample region. This is consistent with a sample-edge/thickness effect (the same effect that, as discussed below, invalidates the thickness measurement), rather than a two-phase separation between an Au film and bare substrate. Critically, no spectral fingerprint—e.g., a plotted transmission spectrum across the 80.8 keV edge for the bright region versus the substrate—was produced to distinguish a genuine Au K-edge from a generic thickness/attenuation boundary. We therefore do NOT claim that the localized region is specifically gold; the localization is of a high-Z/high-attenuation, likely thick, region, and attribution to Au requires an actual edge-fingerprint spectrum. The Edge_SNR map is not a reliable independent corroboration and should not be treated as such. Its population statistics are numerically unstable (Mean ≈ 81,846; Std Dev ≈ 1.76×10^6, with the x-axis extending to ~5×10^7), indicating divide-by-near-zero blow-ups from photon-starved low-count pixels. The spatial map appears legible only because it is clipped to roughly 0–130, and isolated spike pixels are visible along the bottom/left edges. Because this metric is partially driven by the same low-flux artifact that corrupts the edge-step magnitudes, it does not provide clean independent confirmation of a real edge and has been downgraded accordingly. The measurability mask shows solid coverage throughout the upper region and a textbook salt-and-pepper (random 0/1 speckle) pattern in the lower region, which is the expected behavior where the underlying X-ray statistics are marginal. The often-quoted ~75% measurable fraction is misleading, however: this fraction is dominated by the thick upper region where ANY edge/attenuation is present, and the mask does not discriminate Au from generic attenuation. It therefore delineates a thickness/attenuation boundary rather than a gold-specific boundary. ### Thickness Quantification (Not Achieved — Important Caveat) The analysis did NOT succeed in producing a calibrated, trustworthy thickness measurement, and this limitation must be stated plainly. The measured edge-step magnitudes in the upper region (optical-depth jumps of roughly 1.5–2.0) are far too large to be explained by a thin sputtered gold layer at 80.8 keV. A thin sputtered Au film (nm to hundreds of nm) would produce a K-edge jump orders of magnitude too small to detect at 80.8 keV. The oversized values indicate that the measured signal is dominated by confounding effects rather than a pure gold K-edge absorption: these include geometric and thickness-edge effects at the sample boundary, and low-photon-flux (photon-starvation) effects at high energies where the incident X-ray spectrum has very few counts. The provided incident-beam reference spectrum confirms that the flux falls off dramatically above ~70 keV, meaning the high-energy region around the Au edge is inherently photon-starved and prone to inflated, unstable optical-depth estimates. Consequently, the automated thickness calibration correctly failed its internal quality control and was not reported. The edge-step map should therefore be interpreted strictly as a QUALITATIVE localization tool for a high-attenuation region—it reliably tells us WHERE the thick/high-Z material is, but the numerical edge magnitudes cannot be converted into a reliable film thickness without additional corrections (e.g., proper flux normalization, boundary/geometry masking, and possibly energy-response calibration of the detector at high energies). 3. Key Scientific Claims

Claim 1: Spectral X-ray transmission imaging localizes a spatially coherent, high-attenuation (high-Z/thick) region with a well-defined curved boundary; however, without an edge-fingerprint transmission spectrum across 80.8 keV, the localization cannot be attributed specifically to an Au K-edge rather than a generic thickness/geometry boundary, and calibrated thickness cannot be extracted.

Impact: Clarifying that spatial localization by a putative K-edge map may be driven by bulk geometry/thickness and flux effects—rather than the target element's K-edge—highlights the need for explicit spectral validation before claiming element-specific mapping with spectral photon-counting detectors.
