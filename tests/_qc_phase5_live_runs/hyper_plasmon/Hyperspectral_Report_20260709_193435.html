Because the underlying spectra are inherently weak (signal-to-noise ratio near 3.6), a physics-based per-pixel fitting approach was chosen over standard statistical decomposition: at each pixel, the plasmon feature was modeled as a single Lorentzian resonance sitting on a linear background, yielding four coupled physical maps — the resonance energy, the integrated plasmon intensity, the resonance width (FWHM), and a quality flag indicating whether a reliable fit could be obtained. **Reliability of the measurement.** The quality-control map indicates that every analyzed pixel satisfied the acceptance criteria (positive amplitude, physically sensible width, resonance center falling inside the measured energy window, and an acceptable goodness-of-fit). It establishes that a Lorentzian converged at every pixel, but does NOT by itself establish that the recovered parameters are physically meaningful, particularly given the low SNR (~3.6). This boundary pile-up is a classic signature of the fit railing against the energy-window boundary, and should be flagged as a possible fitting artifact rather than a genuine physical population. Consequently, the derived maps — especially the high-energy majority domain — should be treated as tentative in the affected regions. **LSPR energy landscape.** The resonance energy is clustered near a mean of ~0.44 eV with a narrow spread (standard deviation ~0.02 eV) and a full range of roughly 0.40–0.47 eV. Spatially, the energy map is not random but organized into a coherent diagonal gradient: a large, high-energy region (~0.45–0.47 eV) occupies most of the field, transitioning across a diagonal boundary into a lower-energy region (~0.40–0.42 eV) in one corner. Because the LSPR energy of doped-oxide nanocrystals scales with free-carrier density, one plausible interpretation of this gradient is a spatial variation in effective carrier concentration (dopant activation) across the array. This interpretation carries important caveats, however: (i) part of the high-energy domain sits at the top edge of the fitting window, where the fits may be railing rather than reporting a true energy; (ii) in a single-Lorentzian fit at low SNR, energy is coupled to width and amplitude, so the recovered energy is not fully independent; and (iii) the energy gradient is large-scale and does NOT track the individual nanocrystal lattice resolved in the intensity map. This morphological mismatch weakens a purely carrier-density (dopant-activation) story and could equally reflect a specimen-thickness or background-slope gradient across the field. The gradient is therefore best presented as a real spatial trend whose specific physical origin remains uncertain. **Plasmon intensity and array architecture.** The intensity map is spatially structured in a strikingly different way from the energy map: it resolves the individual nanocrystals as discrete square/cuboidal objects arranged in an ordered array, with the strongest integrated plasmon signal concentrated along the edges and junctions between neighboring particles and weaker signal in the particle interiors. This edge-enhanced pattern is consistent with surface plasmon behavior — the induced surface charge and near-field are expected to be maximized at particle boundaries and in the narrow gaps where adjacent nanocrystals nearly touch, consistent with capacitive near-field coupling between neighbors. (Edge enhancement can also partly reflect increased surface-to-volume or geometric projection effects, so the coupling interpretation is presented as consistent-with rather than proven.) The intensity distribution is broad and right-skewed (mean ~224, extending to ~770 a.u.), quantifying this contrast between bright interparticle regions and dimmer particle cores.
