1. Synthesized Scientific Analysis

### Overview and Objective The goal of this study was to detect a gold (Au) coupon within a spectral X-ray transmission image and to estimate its thickness. The measurement leverages the fundamental physics of the Au K-edge — a sharp, element-specific jump in X-ray absorption that occurs at 80.7 keV. Because this absorption jump is unique to gold, measuring its magnitude at every pixel provides both a way to *detect* the presence of gold (wherever the edge is present) and a way to *quantify* its thickness (from the size of the edge jump). ### Method and Data Quality Rather than relying on statistical decomposition (NMF/PCA/ICA) — which is poorly suited to this problem because the signal of interest is a subtle, localized spectral discontinuity rather than a broad spectral 'fingerprint' — the analysis used a direct, physics-based approach. The sample image was divided by a companion flat-field reference image (the I0 baseline, acquired with no sample present) to obtain transmission, and the negative logarithm of transmission yielded the absorbance (the product of the material's attenuation coefficient and its thickness). The size of the absorption jump was measured at every pixel using narrow windows placed immediately below and above the Au K-edge at 80.7 keV — the two windows straddle the edge energy so that their difference isolates the element-specific jump. Working in this high-energy regime is advantageous because the high-energy X-rays penetrate cleanly, but it should be emphasized that the specific windows are defined by their position on either side of 80.7 keV, not by any 40 keV lower bound. Dividing the measured edge jump by the known tabulated jump for gold (based on its standard density of 19.32 g/cm³) converts each pixel into a physical thickness in microns. The underlying data were of medium quality with a healthy overall signal-to-noise ratio (~32.5), and the flat-field reference showed the expected bremsstrahlung continuum shape with characteristic source emission peaks, confirming it is an appropriate normalization baseline. ### Spatial Character of the Maps The edge-step map (Feature 1) returns a measurable gold K-edge signal at essentially every pixel in the field. However, the spatial map itself displays pixel-to-pixel random scatter (salt-and-pepper texture) with no coherent spatial structure, no resolved object, no internal edges, and no visible coupon boundary. The edge-step values cluster around a mean of 0.48 optical-depth units with a narrow spread (standard deviation ≈ 0.08) in a single, well-defined histogram peak. Because the spatial map shows no correlated structure, these data are consistent with a signal fluctuating around a nonzero global mean rather than with a spatially resolved physical object. Importantly, this means nothing was localized in the map: a nonzero edge-step value was simply computed at every pixel. A full-field signal with essentially zero spatial contrast cannot, from these maps alone, be distinguished from a global offset or normalization artifact, and the presence and extent of an actual coupon boundary cannot be determined from this data. A thin border of low or absent signal around the frame edges is plausibly associated with reduced flux at the field margins, but this remains an assumption that cannot be verified from the maps. ### Estimating the Thickness The thickness maps (Features 2 and 3) convert the edge jump into physical units. The primary map (Feature 2) applies a strict measurability gate (positive edge step and signal-to-noise above 3) and returns a mean thickness of approximately 40 microns (39.95 µm) with a spread of roughly 4–6 microns (std ≈ 4.4 µm). The thickness distribution forms a roughly symmetric peak centered near 40 microns. The 'full' map (Feature 3) differs only in its masking and returns a very similar central value (39.55 µm). It should be noted that the thickness maps are a deterministic linear rescaling of the edge-step map (division by the tabulated jump), so their identical spatial texture and matching relative scatter (~10–15%) is an arithmetic consequence and does not constitute an independent cross-check of the result. The 'full' map also retains a small population of near-zero pixels, visible as a minor secondary cluster near 0 microns in the histogram. This may correspond to low-flux or masked border regions, but a histogram spike at zero is a known potential artifact, and the spatial maps do not show a coherent low-value border that would confirm this interpretation. It is therefore noted as a possible edge/masking artifact rather than a confidently explained feature, and these near-zero pixels should be excluded from any thickness estimate. Because the central value rests on the mean of a noisy field with ~10–15% pixel scatter, with no independent standard, no ground-truth comparison, and no spatial validation, the ~40 micron result should be assigned moderate rather than high confidence. The central value may well be correct, but the absolute calibration (tabulated jump, density, detector response) has not been independently checked, and the complete absence of spatial structure means systematic offsets cannot be ruled out. ### Interpretation Taken together, the data are consistent with a gold-bearing signal present across the imaged field at a level corresponding to roughly 40 microns of gold, under the assumed calibration. However, the maps contain no spatial structure that would allow a coupon to be geometrically located or its boundary resolved; only a per-pixel value was computed. The modest pixel-to-pixel scatter (~10–15% of the mean) is consistent with photon-counting statistics at this signal-to-noise level. Whether the gold physically fills the field of view is an experimental-setup claim that would require external confirmation and cannot be established from these maps alone.
