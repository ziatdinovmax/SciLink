1. Synthesized Scientific Analysis

### Overview and Objective The goal of this study was to locate the indium (In), tin (Sn), and lead (Pb) solder deposits within a copper coupon and to map where each element resides. The measurement is a spectral X-ray transmission (radiography) experiment: at every one of 6,400 spatial pixels (an 80×80 grid covering a 20×20 mm field of view), a full transmitted-photon energy spectrum was recorded from a broadband 160 kVp X-ray source. Because each element abruptly changes how strongly it absorbs X-rays at its characteristic 'K-edge' energy (In near 27.9 keV, Sn near 29.2 keV, Pb near 88.0 keV), converting the raw transmission into absorbance and searching for these sudden absorption steps is the physically correct route to identifying and locating each metal. ### What the Statistical Decomposition (NMF) Showed A five-component statistical decomposition of the raw transmission data was performed first. It is important to state up front what this decomposition could and could not do. The model reproduced the measured spectra extremely faithfully — the predicted spectra track the measured spectra almost perfectly across the full energy range, indicating a high-quality, high-confidence reconstruction of the data itself. However, high reconstruction accuracy here does not translate into element identification, because the decomposition was run on raw transmitted counts rather than on absorbance. As a result, the components largely mirror the shape of the X-ray source illumination (a broad bremsstrahlung hump with sharp tungsten-anode characteristic lines near 59 and 67 keV) rather than the absorption fingerprints of the solders. The single most useful result from this decomposition was spatial, not spectral. The first component captured the overall illumination field and its map revealed several discrete, roughly circular dark discs where the transmitted beam is strongly reduced. These dark discs are precisely where thick, heavy-metal solder deposits block the beam, and they mark the regions of interest for element identification. The remaining four components were dominated by a very-low-energy spike and concentrated along the detector edges and in a speckled texture; these represent noise, detector-boundary artifacts, and scatter rather than distinct chemical phases, and they carry low interpretive confidence. In short, the decomposition told us *where the solder blobs are* but not *what they are made of*. ### Physics-Based Element Mapping (Absorption-Edge Analysis) To answer the elemental question directly, a custom physics-based analysis converted the data into absorbance (−ln of transmitted/incident intensity, using the provided no-sample flat-field reference) and then searched each pixel for the tell-tale absorption steps of each metal. The **lead map is the strongest and most reliable result**. Because lead's K-edge sits near 88 keV — far from any other feature in the spectrum — its absorption step is cleanly isolated and unambiguous. The lead map shows two compact, well-defined circular deposits with strong absorption-step magnitudes (up to ~1.25 absorbance units), one in the upper-left region and one in the lower-central region of the field of view. These lead deposits coincide spatially with two of the prominent dark discs seen in the decomposition map, providing independent, cross-validated confirmation that these particular blobs are lead. This is a high-confidence identification. The **indium/tin analysis is more challenging and carries lower confidence**. The In and Sn K-edges lie only about 1.3 keV apart (27.9 vs 29.2 keV), which is near the practical limit of the detector's ~0.2 keV energy resolution. Rather than attempting a crude subtraction, a joint absorption-model fit was used to try to separate the two elements by the distinct shapes of their absorption jumps. This approach recovered spatially coherent, above-noise regions — arc-shaped and compact features in the upper and left portions of the field — that are consistent with genuine light-solder deposits. However, the fit could not cleanly disentangle the two nearly overlapping edges, and the dedicated tin map did not pass internal quality checks. Consequently, the regions labeled as 'In' should be regarded as *low-Z solder (In and/or Sn)* rather than confirmed pure indium; some unresolved tin contamination is likely mixed into these regions. ### Unified Physical Model Bringing all scales of analysis together yields a consistent and coherent picture of the coupon. The sample consists of a copper background hosting several discrete high-density solder deposits that appear as transmission-deficit discs. These deposits fall into two physically distinct classes: 1. **Heavy-metal (lead) deposits** — two large, compact, high-absorbance circular blobs (upper-left and lower-center) that are confidently identified by their isolated, unambiguous 88 keV absorption edge. These are the most trustworthy identifications in the study. 2. **Light-metal (indium/tin) deposits** — smaller, arc-shaped and compact features (upper and left regions) identified by their near-overlapping ~28–29 keV absorption edges. These are confidently solder material, but the specific split between indium and tin cannot be resolved at the available energy resolution, so they are best reported as combined In/Sn regions pending finer-resolution measurement. The statistical decomposition and the physics-based edge maps agree on *where* the deposits sit — the dark discs, the lead blobs, and the light-metal features occupy mutually consistent locations — which strengthens confidence in the overall spatial architecture. The vast majority of the field (mean element-assignment near zero, with only a few hundred pixels flagged as containing any solder) is bare copper background, consistent with sparse, localized solder placement on the coupon. ### Confidence Summary and Recommendations - **High confidence:** Location of all solder deposits as transmission-deficit discs; identification of the two lead deposits via the isolated 88 keV edge. - **Moderate-to-low confidence:** Assignment of the light-metal regions specifically to indium; these should be treated as combined In/Sn until resolved. - **Recommendation:** A repeat measurement with finer energy binning (well below 0.2 keV) across the 27–30 keV window, or complementary spectroscopy, would be needed to definitively separate indium from tin. 3. Key Scientific Claims

Claim 1: In spectral X-ray transmission imaging of multi-metal solder samples, lead deposits can be confidently and unambiguously localized through their spatially isolated high-energy K-edge, whereas indium and tin deposits with closely spaced K-edges cannot be reliably separated at moderate detector energy resolution.

Impact: Establishing the practical resolution limits for distinguishing elements with near-degenerate absorption edges directly informs detector selection and measurement design for non-destructive elemental mapping of electronic solders and other multi-metal assemblies.

Literature Search Query: Has anyone quantified the energy resolution required to separate elements with closely spaced K-edges in energy-resolved X-ray transmission imaging of metal solder joints?

Claim 2: Converting raw spectral X-ray transmission data into absorbance and performing K-edge step analysis provides element-specific spatial maps that statistical decomposition of raw transmission counts alone cannot deliver, even when the decomposition reconstructs the data with high fidelity.

Impact: Demonstrating that high reconstruction accuracy in blind statistical decomposition does not guarantee chemically meaningful phase separation guides analysts toward physics-based absorption modeling for reliable elemental identification in spectral imaging.
